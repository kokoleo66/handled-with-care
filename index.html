Do They Need More Help?
L
A guide for deciding on assisted living or memory care

Top Signs It May Be Time for More Support

  • Medication mix-ups: pills are missed, doubled, or taken at the wrong times despite reminders and pill organizers.
  • Frequent falls or near-falls: repeated loss of balance, unexplained bruises, or a new fear of walking without support.
  • Weight loss or skipped meals: empty fridge, expired food, clothes getting loose, or reliance on simple, unhealthy snacks instead of cooked meals.
  • Decline in hygiene and self-care: difficulty bathing, dressing properly, body odor, or wearing dirty clothes; home becomes noticeably unclean.
  • Memory lapses that affect safety: getting lost in familiar places, leaving the stove on, wandering, or repeated questions and confusion about time or place.
  • Missed appointments and neglected health: forgetting doctor visits, ignoring new symptoms, or avoiding needed medical care.
  • Household neglect and safety hazards: piles of mail, clutter, unpaid bills, spoiled food, or hazards like burnt pots and tripping risks.
  • Social withdrawal and mood changes: pulling away from friends, losing interest in hobbies, signs of depression, anxiety, or agitation.
  • Caregiver burnout or inconsistent care: family caregivers are exhausted, missing their own appointments, losing sleep, or feeling resentful; care routines become unreliable.
  • Repeated hospital or ER visits: back and forth trips for falls, infections, medication errors, or other issues that could be prevented with closer supervision.
When memory care specifically may be needed: persistent wandering, aggressive or highly agitated episodes, moderate to severe cognitive impairment on testing, or repeated safety incidents that standard assisted living cannot safely manage.

    If medications are the main problem ▼
    • Ask the pharmacy about blister packs, med sync, or delivery.
    • Consider a locked med dispenser with alarms or a service that pre-sorts weekly doses.
    If falls or mobility are the main problem ▼
    • Request a physical therapy evaluation for strength, balance, and proper assistive devices.
    • Add grab bars, better lighting, and remove loose rugs; consider a medical alert button or fall detection device.
    If memory or safety are the main problem ▼
    • Ask for a cognitive evaluation (Mini-Cog, MoCA, or similar) through their doctor or a memory clinic.
    • If there is wandering, stove issues, or getting lost, start memory care conversations immediately. Standard assisted living may not be enough.
    If caregiver burnout is the main problem ▼
    • Be honest with the doctor: "I can't keep this up safely." That alone can unlock resources, referrals, and urgency.
    • Look into respite care (short-term stays) or adult day programs for regular breaks.
    If money is the sticking point ▼
    • Contact your local Area Agency on Aging or Aging and Disability Resource Center for free or low-cost counseling on benefits, Medicaid waivers, and veteran's aid.
    • Ask facilities directly what they accept (private pay, long-term care insurance, Medicaid, VA benefits) before you tour.
